What Depth Wizard does — and what it doesn’t

Depth Wizard turns satellite imagery into an interactive 3D terrain view. The terrain you see is always a real digital elevation model (DEM). Satellite imagery is draped on it as texture, and the output of a depth model (Depth Anything V2) is shown only as a labelled relative-depth visualisation — never as measured height.

The three pages

1

Workbench

  • Search any place by name (OpenStreetMap geocoding).
  • Search for scene queries the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem live for real Sentinel-2 L2A scenes at that location, filtered by date range and cloud cover, and previews the true-colour scene you pick.
  • Upload image previews a PNG/JPG of your own.
  • Start generation plays the pipeline’s steps. It is a prototype: the relative-depth, elevation, DSM and 3D panels show the Darjeeling reference results whatever scene you picked or uploaded. It is not a live reconstruction of your scene.
2

Explore

  • Four real 10 × 10 km regions in West Bengal — Darjeeling, Kolkata, Bardhaman and Sundarbans — each with Sentinel-2 imagery, DAv2 relative depth and a DEM.
  • Layers: satellite, relative depth and elevation (flat); DSM, DEM elevation and true colour (3D). The stats strip shows each region’s real numbers.
  • Run reconstruction replays the pipeline log, including real research findings. Flythrough is a scripted camera move; drag, scroll and pinch to orbit and zoom.

Where the heights come from

Every 3D surface in the app is a DEM. The three plains regions use Copernicus GLO-30 at 30 m, so they read less detailed than Darjeeling — that is the data, not a bug.

Relative depth is frozen Depth Anything V2 (ViT-Large). It orders pixels near-to-far; it has no metres. On nadir satellite imagery it does not reliably track elevation: on Darjeeling its correlation with the DEM flipped from +0.60 to −0.41 once the broad spatial trend was removed.

What the research found

Alongside the app, a research track tested whether real metric height can be predicted from imagery. The short answer depends on resolution.

SENTINEL-2 · 10 m · IN THE APP

DEM only

Across 32 Indian test tiles, nothing tested on top of the imagery (frequency fusion, random-forest and linear residuals, CNN corrections, a fine-tuned depth model, a ranking loss) beat simply using the DEM. The best terrain DEM is FABDEM — median error 1.78 m against ICESat-2 ground photons — and the best surface DEM is Copernicus GLO-30 (4.48 m median against canopy-top segments). A second coarse sensor (Landsat, 15 m) behaved the same way.

VERY HIGH RESOLUTION · ~0.3 m · RESEARCH ONLY

Predicted height, gated on imagery

On 0.3 m imagery (the DFC2019 benchmark) a fine-tuned Depth Anything V2 does beat a strong per-tile baseline (MAE 1.98 m, RMSE 3.49 m). An offline pipeline builds DSM = FABDEM + predicted height above ground and was run on 6 Maxar crops in Sikkim and Darjeeling. It is not in the app. It under-states tall canopy (a ceiling around 18–23 m), it did not generalise to US aerial imagery, and it has only been tested on mountain terrain.

In synthetic tests, a model trained on imagery coarsened to 5 m still clearly beat the baseline; from 6 m on it no longer did. That is why learned height is tied to very-high-resolution imagery and not to Sentinel-2.
